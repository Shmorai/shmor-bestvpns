Privacy

No-logs VPNs: what it means and how to check

When you use a VPN, the provider can see what your internet provider used to see. A no-logs policy is its promise not to record that. Here’s how to tell a strong promise from a marketing line.

Photo: Taylor Vick on Unsplash

On this page

  1. The kinds of data a VPN could log
  2. How independent audits work
  3. Infrastructure that supports no-logs
  4. Jurisdiction and ownership

The kinds of data a VPN could log

  • Activity logs: websites visited, DNS requests, files downloaded. A privacy VPN should never keep these.
  • Connection logs: your real IP address, connection times, the server used, data transferred. Strong no-logs VPNs avoid storing anything that links a session to you.
  • Account data: email, payment records and support tickets. Most providers keep some of this to run the service. A few let you sign up with only an account number and pay in cash or cryptocurrency.
  • Aggregate data: such as total load on a server, which can’t identify individuals.

How independent audits work

Several providers have hired outside security and accounting firms to check their no-logs claims. Audits vary a lot, so read the report or summary and check:

  • Scope: was it the privacy policy, the server infrastructure, the apps, or all of them?
  • Date: an audit is a snapshot. Recent and repeated audits are more reassuring.
  • Access: did auditors inspect live servers and configurations, or rely on interviews and documents?

Real-world events can also be evidence: some providers have had servers seized or received legal demands and been able to show that no user data was stored.

Infrastructure that supports no-logs

  • RAM-only (diskless) servers: run from memory, so data is wiped on reboot and nothing is written to a hard drive.
  • Owned vs rented servers: providers that control their hardware rely less on third-party data centres.
  • Open-source apps: anyone can inspect the code.

Jurisdiction and ownership

A VPN company must follow the laws where it’s based and where its servers are. Some people prefer providers outside the “Five Eyes”, “Nine Eyes” or “Fourteen Eyes” intelligence-sharing alliances, though a genuinely log-free service has little to hand over wherever it’s based. Ownership matters too: check who owns the brand, because several well-known VPNs share a parent company.

Frequently asked questions

Are no-logs VPNs really no-logs?
You can’t verify it yourself, which is why independent audits, RAM-only servers, transparency reports and real-world tests such as court cases matter. Treat unaudited claims with caution.
Can the police get my data from a VPN?
They can request it with the right legal process. What they get depends on what the provider stores. A provider that keeps no activity or connection logs has little or nothing to hand over.
Is a VPN enough to protect my privacy?
No. Combine it with a privacy-friendly browser, tracker blocking, strong unique passwords, two-factor authentication and care about what you share while logged in to accounts.
